OVERVIEW

Learning Engineering Through Field Work

Before working primarily in simulation and mechanical design, I developed several years of hands-on construction experience with NorCal Concrete & Landscape.

My work involved supporting concrete placement, hardscape installation, masonry and retaining structures, site preparation, material handling, and finish work across residential and commercial projects.

This experience gave me practical exposure to how dimensions, tolerances, material behavior, sequencing, and accessibility affect whether a design can actually be built efficiently in the field.

ENGINEERING RELEVANCE

Understanding What Makes a Design Buildable

Field construction taught me that even a technically correct design can become difficult to execute if dimensions, access, installation order, material behavior, or tool clearance are not considered early.

That experience now influences how I approach CAD and mechanical design. I pay greater attention to manufacturability, assembly, service access, tolerances, and how components will actually be handled by the person building or maintaining them.

The connection between field work and engineering design has been especially useful when evaluating mechanical components intended for fabrication, assembly, and real-world service conditions.

PRACTICAL TAKEAWAYS

What Hands-On Work Taught Me

Constructability Matters

Geometry has to account for how materials, tools, and people physically access and assemble a structure.

Small Errors Accumulate

Layout, level, spacing, and alignment errors early in a project can create much larger problems during finishing and installation.

Materials Behave Differently in Practice

Real materials introduce variability that is often simplified in drawings, models, and idealized calculations.

Design Should Support the Builder

Good engineering documentation should make fabrication and installation clearer, faster, and less error-prone.

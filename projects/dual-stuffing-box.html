Dual Stuffing
Box

Mechanical design of a serviceable dual-packing assembly for continuous tubing applications, developed around field-maintenance, pressure-containment, and manufacturing constraints.

Fusion 360 Mechanical Design DFM Technical Drawings FEA

OVERVIEW

Designing for serviceability in the field.

The project focused on developing a dual-packing stuffing-box architecture for equipment using continuous tubing.

Unlike conventional jointed rod systems, continuous tubing cannot simply be disconnected and pulled through a conventional packing assembly during maintenance.

This created a core design requirement: packing components had to be accessible laterally so that sealing elements could be serviced without removing the tubing from the top of the assembly.

MY ROLE

From concept development to detailed CAD.

I developed the component architecture and detailed CAD geometry in Fusion 360, including the main body, side-access region, threaded interfaces, connection geometry, and service-access features.

The design process required balancing packaging constraints, packing clearance, structural wall thickness, access-panel geometry, and machining considerations.

I also produced technical drawings with sectional views, dimensions, and threaded-interface callouts to communicate the design for manufacturing review.

DESIGN DECISION

Why a side-access door?

Traditional packing assemblies are commonly serviced by removing components vertically along the rod or tubing axis.

Continuous tubing changes that maintenance constraint because the tubing may extend for thousands of feet without an intermediate connection that can simply be disconnected.

The resulting solution was a lateral-access concept paired with split packing, allowing sealing components to be replaced from the side rather than passed over the tubing.

STRUCTURAL VALIDATION

Designing around a 5,000 PSI requirement.

The access panel introduces a structural discontinuity into the pressure-containing body, making the door thickness, fastener arrangement, and surrounding wall geometry important design variables.

The design process included planning static-stress finite element analysis to evaluate local stresses and determine efficient panel dimensions while maintaining the required structural margin.

This connected the CAD-development process directly to simulation-driven design rather than treating analysis as a separate final step.

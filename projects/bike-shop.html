OVERVIEW

Diagnosing mechanical problems in the real world.

From 2024 to 2026, I worked as a Bicycle Technician at the UC Santa Barbara Associated Students Bike Shop.

A large part of the role involved helping students and staff troubleshoot mechanical problems on their bicycles. I would inspect the system, reproduce the reported issue, identify likely causes, perform the necessary service, and verify the repair before returning the bicycle to the rider.

I also explained mechanical problems, component wear, and maintenance techniques to riders so they could better understand how their bicycles worked and recognize issues before they became larger failures.

SHOP OPERATIONS

Supporting the entire repair workflow.

My responsibilities extended beyond individual repairs. I supported inventory operations, component replenishment, and shop-floor tool organization so technicians could work efficiently.

As part of the Data & Metrics Team, I also tracked repair throughput and turnaround times to help identify workflow bottlenecks and operational constraints.

Working in a service environment required balancing repair quality, available parts, technician time, and rider needs while maintaining an organized and functional workspace.

ENGINEERING CONNECTION

Understanding mechanical systems through repair.

Working on real mechanical assemblies made concepts such as alignment, friction, preload, wear, tolerances, and serviceability much more tangible.

Small changes in component condition or adjustment could produce significant differences in system behavior, reinforcing the importance of careful inspection and root-cause diagnosis.

This experience now influences how I approach mechanical design. I consider not only whether a component can perform its intended function, but also how it will be assembled, accessed, adjusted, maintained, and eventually replaced.

PRACTICAL TAKEAWAYS

What bicycle service taught me.

Diagnose the Root Cause

The component showing a symptom is not always the component responsible for the problem.

Mechanical Details Matter

Alignment, wear, lubrication, preload, and adjustment can have significant effects on system performance.

Serviceability Matters

Mechanical systems are easier to maintain when designers account for access, adjustment, inspection, and component replacement.

Technical Communication Matters

A successful repair also requires explaining the problem and solution clearly to the person using the system.
